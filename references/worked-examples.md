# Worked Examples

## When to Consult

Use when a formula, transformation, or procedure would be hard to reproduce from the final answer alone.

## Source Summary

Sweller and Cooper studied algebra learning in five experiments. Worked examples helped learners acquire solution patterns, with benefits especially on problems similar to those studied. Results on structurally different problems were more limited. The experiments involved learning and test activities; they did not establish that reading any explanation produces broad expertise.

## Application to This Skill

- Select a short, meaningful part of the user's actual task. Include the starting information, important steps, and result.
- Connect each step to its purpose, so the explanation teaches more than an ordered list of operations.
- Keep a useful check beside the example. Label simplified or hypothetical data, and omit mechanics the user already understands.

## Original Example

Fictional task: calculate the combined conversion rate for two campaigns.

| Campaign | Conversions | Visits | Rate |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A | 20 | 100 | 20% |
| B | 9 | 900 | 1% |

> Add conversions to obtain 29 successes, then add visits to obtain 1,000 opportunities. Divide 29 by 1,000: the combined rate is 2.9%. Averaging 20% and 1% would give 10.5%, incorrectly giving equal weight to campaigns with different traffic. To check the calculation, multiply 2.9% by 1,000; it returns 29 conversions. This checks the arithmetic, not whether the source data count visits consistently.

The example supplies the full result without asking the user to solve a problem first.

## Limits

A worked example is not proof that the user can solve a new problem. The algebra findings do not directly validate this analytics example or the skill.

## Source Record

John Sweller and Graham A. Cooper. 1985. *The Use of Worked Examples as a Substitute for Problem Solving in Learning Algebra*. Cognition and Instruction, 2(1), 59–89. DOI: 10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3. Relevant sections: Experiments 2–5 and General Discussion.
